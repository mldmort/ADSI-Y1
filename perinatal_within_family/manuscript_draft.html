Within-family effects of perinatal adversity on autism traits: neonatal intensive care, preterm birth and early growth difficulty in SPARK and the Simons Simplex Collection

Draft v0.2, November 16, 2026. Authors to be added. Analysis code: ADSI-Y1/perinatal_within_family (Expanse).

Abstract

Background. Perinatal complications such as preterm birth and neonatal intensive care are consistently associated with autism, but most estimates compare unrelated children and are open to confounding by factors shared within families, including parental genotype, socioeconomic circumstances and reporting style. Sibling comparisons remove this shared confounding.

Methods. We analysed 21,049 children of European genetic ancestry with the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) in three SPARK sequencing cohorts (13,674 families, 6,754 with two or more analysed children), 14,055 of them autistic. Three parent-reported perinatal exposures from the basic medical screening form (neonatal intensive care or oxygen at birth, premature birth, difficulty gaining weight) were entered jointly into a hybrid mixed model that decomposes each exposure into its family mean (between-family effect) and the child's deviation from that mean (within-family effect, identified by sibling contrasts), adjusting for sex, birth year, autism status, ancestry principal components, sequencing covariates and the child's genetic burden: tiers of de novo and inherited loss-of-function and missense variants, and polygenic scores for autism, gestational duration and preterm birth. Estimates were combined across cohorts by inverse-variance meta-analysis. In the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC), where the perinatal interview covers probands only, we tested ten perinatal items against SCQ, SRS, Vineland-II and CBCL 6-18 scores in 1,305 to 1,752 probands.

Results. All three exposures were associated with higher SCQ between families (NICU/oxygen +0.06 SD, premature birth +0.07 SD, difficulty gaining weight +0.22 SD). Within families, only NICU/oxygen retained its effect (+0.09 SD, 95% CI +0.02 to +0.16; p = 0.01), which was unchanged when multiple births were excluded, when the analysis was restricted to term births, and in all ancestries combined. The within-family effect of premature birth was null (-0.01 SD, p = 0.9; between minus within +0.08, p = 0.02) despite a tetrachoric correlation of 0.68 with NICU/oxygen, and the within-family effect of difficulty gaining weight was positive but imprecise (+0.06 SD, p = 0.13; between minus within +0.16, p = 0.001). Adjusting for the child's measured genetic burden changed no exposure coefficient by more than 0.004 SD; within families, SCQ rose with loss-of-function de novo mutations in constrained genes (+0.12 SD per tier-1 variant, p = 0.01; +0.09 SD per tier-2 variant, p = 0.02) and not with negative-control variant classes. In SSC probands, oxygen at birth and shorter gestation were associated with higher SRS scores, and no perinatal item was associated with SCQ, Vineland-II or CBCL scores.

Conclusions. The population association between perinatal adversity and autistic traits is largely a between-family phenomenon. Neonatal intensive care or oxygen at birth is the one exposure with a within-family effect on SCQ, independent of prematurity and of the child's rare and polygenic burden, pointing to a direct or birth-specific contribution of the neonatal course to social-communication traits.

Introduction

Preterm birth, low birth weight, neonatal intensive care and other perinatal complications are among the most reproducible non-genetic correlates of autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Meta-analyses of cohort and registry studies report relative risks of about 1.3 to 1.5 for preterm birth and for a range of neonatal complications, with a dose-response relation to gestational age. Whether these associations reflect a causal contribution of the perinatal course, or confounding by factors that make some families both more likely to experience perinatal complications and more likely to have autistic children, remains unresolved. Candidate shared factors include parental age, socioeconomic circumstances, maternal health and the parents' own genetic liability to autism, which influences both the child's genotype and the prenatal environment.

Sibling comparisons address this problem directly: siblings share half their genome, the same parents and the same household, so a within-family contrast between an exposed and an unexposed sibling removes all confounding by factors constant within the family. The hybrid, or Mundlak, mixed model estimates the within-family effect alongside the between-family effect in one model and tests their difference, which is a direct test of family-level confounding. Registry studies of ASD diagnosis have applied sibling designs to preterm birth with mixed results, but no study has done so with quantitative autism traits, with several perinatal exposures entered jointly, and with the child's own measured genetic burden in the model.

The SPARK cohort provides the elements needed: tens of thousands of families with more than one sequenced child, a parent-completed medical screening form that records neonatal intensive care, prematurity and early growth difficulty for autistic and non-autistic children alike, the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) as a quantitative trait, and exome or genome sequencing from which de novo and inherited rare-variant burden and polygenic scores can be derived. Here we estimate the within- and between-family effects of three perinatal exposures on SCQ in SPARK, test whether the within-family effects are explained by the child's genetic burden, examine their robustness across sex, ancestry, multiple births and gestational age, and extend the analysis to five outcomes in the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC).

Methods

Cohorts and participants

SPARK children were drawn from three non-overlapping sequencing cohorts: the exome-sequenced cohort (WES), the 2023 whole-genome release (WGS) and the 2026_08 whole-genome release (WGS 2026_08); children present in two releases were analysed once. The primary analysis used children of European genetic ancestry (predicted from genotype principal components), with all ancestries combined as a sensitivity analysis. Children were included if they had a parent-completed SCQ, were in the de novo mutation call set with an acceptable mutation count, and had complete data on every model variable. Table 1 gives the analysed samples.

SSC families (one autistic proband, unaffected siblings and both parents, whole-genome sequenced) were analysed for the proband-only medical history interview; because that interview was not administered for siblings, SSC exposure effects are population estimates with one proband per family.

Perinatal exposures

In SPARK the exposures come from the basic medical screening form, a parent-completed checklist covering every registered child: "needed NICU care or oxygen at birth", "born prematurely" and "difficulty gaining weight" (a growth item, not birth weight), each coded 1 if ticked and 0 if the form was completed without the tick. The three items were entered jointly in every model so that each coefficient is adjusted for the other two. Their pairwise tetrachoric correlations (the correlation of the latent continuous liabilities assumed to underlie the binary items) were estimated by maximum likelihood with the bivariate normal distribution, with phi coefficients and co-occurrence counts alongside.

In SSC the perinatal items come from the medical history interview of probands: gestational age in weeks (and preterm birth as under 37 weeks), birth weight, NICU admission, oxygen at birth, respiratory distress, hyperbilirubinemia, 1-minute Apgar below 7, Caesarean section and induced labour. Binary items with fewer than 40 exposed probands were not analysed.

Outcomes

The SCQ total score was standardised within cohort (SD units). In SSC the outcomes were the SCQ, the parent-report SRS raw total, the Vineland-II adaptive behaviour composite and the CBCL 6-18 internalizing and externalizing T scores, each standardised within the cohort.

Genetic variables

De novo mutations were called in each cohort and annotated into tiers. Loss-of-function (LoF) mutations were tiered by the GeneBayes s_het of the gene (tier 1: s_het at least 0.18; tier 2: 0.03 to 0.18; tier 3: below 0.03 or unscored). Missense mutations were tiered by the number of four predictors (ClinPred, AlphaMissense, popEVE, MPC) calling them damaging (tier 1: four; tier 2: three; tier 3: two). Synonymous de novo mutations were retained as a negative control. For the 2026_08 release, whose indel calls are noisier, single-nucleotide tiers were used and no synonymous count was available. Inherited rare variants (cohort and gnomAD allele frequency below 0.001, the child's own de novo sites excluded) were tiered on the same scheme. All tier counts entered the models as raw counts, so that each coefficient is the change in outcome per additional variant of that tier; sex-chromosome mutations were not counted.

Polygenic scores were computed from genome-wide summary statistics with SBayesRC (autism, SSC-excluded iPSYCH weights) or PRS-CS (gestational duration and preterm birth, fetal EGG consortium GWAS), standardised within cohort and residualised on ten ancestry principal components and genotyping array.

Statistical models

Hybrid mixed model (SPARK). For child i in family j,

SCQ_ij = sum_k [bW_k (x_kij - xbar_kj) + bB_k xbar_kj] + g' z_j + u_j + e_ij,

where x_k are the child-level predictors (the three exposures, the genetic terms, sex, birth year and autism status), xbar_kj is the family mean of x_k over the analysed children, z_j are covariates entered without decomposition (ten principal components, array or source indicators, sequencing coverage), u_j is a family random intercept and e_ij the residual. bW_k is the within-family effect, identified only by families with two or more analysed children in which the children differ on x_k; it equals the family fixed-effects estimate. bB_k is the between-family effect, the association of the family mean of x_k with the family mean of SCQ. The difference bB_k minus bW_k is a Wald test of family-level confounding. Models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (lme4) with CR2 family-clustered standard errors (clubSandwich). Each cohort was fitted separately and coefficients were pooled by fixed-effect inverse-variance meta-analysis, with Cochran's Q for heterogeneity.

Attenuation by genetic burden. The main model was refitted without the genetic terms on the same children (M0); the change in each exposure coefficient measures how much of the association is accounted for by the measured genetic burden.

Sensitivity analyses. The main model was refitted (i) in all ancestries combined; (ii) in boys and girls separately; (iii) excluding multiple births; (iv) in term births only (the prematurity term dropped), which isolates NICU/oxygen and growth difficulty from gestational age; and (v) in autistic children only and in non-autistic siblings only.

SSC probands. Each outcome was regressed on each perinatal item with ordinary least squares and HC1 standard errors, adjusting for sex, birth year, ten principal components and coverage (M0), and additionally for the full genetic block (M3) on the same children. A genetics-only model per outcome gave the genetic effects.

Results

Samples and exposure co-occurrence

The main SPARK analysis comprised 21,049 children with SCQ in 13,674 families, of whom 14,129 children in 6,754 families had at least one analysed sibling and therefore contributed to the within-family estimates (Table 1). NICU or oxygen at birth was reported for 1,187 children, premature birth for 2,233 and difficulty gaining weight for 669. NICU/oxygen and premature birth co-occurred far more often than expected: 685 children had both, and the tetrachoric correlation of the two items was 0.68 (phi 0.36), whereas difficulty gaining weight was only weakly related to either (tetrachoric 0.28 and 0.29; Figure 1B).

Within- and between-family effects on SCQ

All three exposures were associated with higher SCQ between families (Figure 1A): a family in which all analysed children had NICU/oxygen scored +0.06 SD higher (95% CI +0.01 to +0.11; p = 0.01) than one in which none did, with corresponding between-family effects of +0.07 SD for premature birth (p = 1e-4) and +0.22 SD for difficulty gaining weight (p = 7e-12). The within-family estimates diverged. The sibling with NICU/oxygen scored +0.09 SD higher than the sibling without (95% CI +0.02 to +0.16; p = 0.01), an effect no smaller than the between-family one (difference -0.02, p = 0.6). Premature birth had no within-family effect (-0.01 SD, 95% CI -0.06 to +0.05; p = 0.9), and the gap to its between-family effect was significant (+0.08, p = 0.02), indicating that the population association of prematurity with SCQ arises from family-level factors. Difficulty gaining weight had a within-family effect of +0.06 SD (95% CI -0.02 to +0.14; p = 0.13), about a quarter of its between-family effect (difference +0.16, p = 0.001); the point estimate is similar in size to the NICU effect, but the exposure is rarer (669 children) and the interval includes zero. Cohort-specific within-family estimates were homogeneous (Table S1).

Sensitivity analyses

The within-family effect of NICU/oxygen was stable across specifications (Figure 1C): +0.09 SD (n = 30,252; p = 0.01) in all ancestries combined, +0.09 SD when multiple births were excluded (p = 0.03), and +0.06 SD in term births only (p = 0.19), where the absence of premature-born children removes a third of exposed children but the estimate is unchanged in size. Estimates in boys (+0.09 SD) and girls (+0.04 SD) did not differ. Stratifying by autism status left the within-family contrasts underpowered (multiplex families with two analysed autistic children: 1,190; families with two analysed non-autistic children: 122), with imprecise estimates of +0.07 SD in autistic children and +0.34 SD in non-autistic siblings. Premature birth was null in every specification, and difficulty gaining weight remained between +0.05 and +0.08 SD except in girls, reaching nominal significance in all ancestries combined (+0.07 SD, p = 0.044).

Genetic burden does not account for the exposure effects

Removing the sixteen genetic terms from the model (M0) changed the exposure coefficients by at most 0.004 SD (NICU/oxygen within-family +0.084 without and +0.088 with the genetic block). In the full model (Figure 1D-E) the within-family SCQ gradient of rare variation was carried by loss-of-function de novo mutations in constrained genes, +0.12 SD per tier-1 variant (95% CI +0.02 to +0.22; p = 0.01) and +0.09 SD per tier-2 variant (95% CI +0.01 to +0.16; p = 0.02), with the two damaging missense tiers near zero and all five negative-control classes within 0.04 SD of zero. Inherited tiers had smaller within-family effects (tier-1 LoF +0.05 SD per variant, p = 0.06). The autism polygenic score had a within-family effect of +0.018 SD per SD (p = 0.07) and the fetal gestational-duration score +0.022 SD per SD (p = 0.03); the preterm-birth score was null. Neither gestational score predicted the perinatal exposures themselves within families (data from the preceding analyses), so they cannot mediate the exposure effects.

SSC probands

Among SSC probands (Figure 2A), no perinatal item was associated with SCQ, Vineland-II or the CBCL externalizing score. Oxygen at birth (+0.11 SD, 95% CI +0.01 to +0.20; p = 0.04; 147 exposed of 1,721) and shorter gestation (-0.017 SD per week, p = 0.02) were associated with higher SRS scores, and induced labour with higher CBCL internalizing scores (+0.10 SD, p = 0.04). None of these changed by more than 0.004 SD when the genetic block was added. The genetic panel (Figure 2B) showed the expected direction for the Vineland-II composite, which fell by 0.19 SD per tier-1 LoF de novo mutation (p = 6e-4) and by 0.08 SD per tier-2 variant (p = 0.06), while the SCQ rose with inherited tier-2 LoF burden (+0.07 SD per variant, p = 0.001); negative-control classes were null throughout.

Discussion

Three perinatal exposures that are all associated with autistic traits in the population behave differently once siblings are compared. Neonatal intensive care or oxygen at birth retains its full effect within families, is unchanged by removing multiple births or restricting to term births, and is not explained by the child's de novo, inherited or polygenic burden. Premature birth, which co-occurs with NICU care in most exposed children, has no within-family effect at all; its population association with SCQ is entirely attributable to factors shared by siblings. Difficulty gaining weight sits between the two, with a within-family estimate of two thirds the NICU effect but an interval that includes zero.

The contrast between NICU/oxygen and prematurity is informative because the two items are so strongly correlated (tetrachoric 0.68). If the NICU effect were a proxy for being born early, it should attenuate in term births and in the joint model; it does neither. The item instead captures the severity of the neonatal course, including respiratory compromise, which has independent associations with neurodevelopment in preterm cohorts, and the within-family design shows that this is not a reflection of family-level risk. The null within-family effect of prematurity, in turn, is consistent with registry sibling studies in which the association between moderate prematurity and ASD diagnosis attenuates substantially after sibling comparison, and suggests that the family-level factors behind the population association (parental age, maternal health, socioeconomic circumstances, and parental genotype acting on the prenatal environment) are what link preterm birth to autism in unrelated-child designs.

The measured genetic burden of the child does not account for any of the exposure effects, within or between families, and the gestational and preterm polygenic scores, which capture the fetal genetic contribution to gestational length, had no effect on the exposures and only marginal effects on SCQ. Genetic confounding through the child's own genotype is therefore not the explanation for the between-family effects; parental genotype acting through the prenatal environment remains possible and is what the sibling design controls.

The SSC results are limited by the proband-only design and by sample sizes of 1,300 to 1,750, which cannot detect effects of the size seen in SPARK for most items, but they add two observations: oxygen at birth and shorter gestation relate to SRS scores in the expected direction, and the genetic block behaves as expected, with constrained LoF de novo mutations lowering the Vineland-II composite in autistic children.

Limitations. The SPARK exposures are parent-reported checklist items without gestational age for most children, which precludes dose-response analysis; the exposed-sibling counts limit precision for difficulty gaining weight and for the sex- and diagnosis-stratified analyses; SCQ is a screening instrument and in SPARK was completed at registration; and the within-family design does not remove confounding by factors that differ between siblings, such as birth order and maternal age at birth, of which birth year is the available proxy. The genetic tiers other than loss-of-function de novo mutations are underpowered for single-tier inference and are included to adjust the exposure effects and to show the expected gradient.

Conclusion. Among perinatal adversities, neonatal intensive care or oxygen at birth has a within-family effect on social-communication traits that is independent of prematurity and of the child's genetic burden, whereas the association of preterm birth with autistic traits is a between-family phenomenon.
